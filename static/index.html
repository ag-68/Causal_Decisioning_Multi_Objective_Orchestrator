Causal decisioning

Closed-loop message orchestrator

Get git repo

A shop, a music app, or a delivery app can send a note: a buzz on the phone, an email waiting in the inbox, or a short clip inside the app. The note can bring a sale or a visit. The same note can make a person say “stop writing to me.”

Message Orchestration is about the decision each morning: who should hear something, and who should be left alone. Content decisioning is the same choice for a song or a post: once some other model has scored it, decide whether to distribute it, show it less, hold it for review, or leave it alone.

— Greedy rules prioritize messages/people with highest buy-chance. Often they choose a loud message for people who would buy anyway, missing opportunities on persuadable buyers and increasing tiredness (message exposure) of the population. —

Our Approach:

Optimize, for each person, the note that adds the most extra sales, while the extra stops caused by those notes stay inside the day's limit. Each morning the charge on extra stops is refined from yesterday's value to match the population's new tiredness.

A welcome note, or too many notes

One buzz: “your order is ready.” Often welcome.
A second and a third the same week. Now it feels like nagging.
After enough of them, the person blocks the number.

If we only chase today’s sales, we buzz almost everyone. People who would have come back anyway get a note they did not need. People who hate being interrupted leave for good, and next week the notes stop working.

Two house rules for the whole morning

Out of 1,000 people, the shop allows about 15 to say “stop.”

And at most about 300 phones may buzz. The rest get a quieter note, or nothing.

Each person still gets their own decision. Ann may get the buzz because, for her, it is likely to cause a visit. Ben is left alone because he would have bought anyway. The rules only limit the day’s totals, the way a café will not seat 300 people when it has 80 chairs.

A buzz leaves the person more tired. A quiet day lets that tiredness fade. Tomorrow we choose again, looking at the new tiredness. We do not pretend every morning is the first morning.

What “causal” means here

We count only the extra sale caused by the note, compared with sending nothing to the same person. Someone who would have bought anyway is not a win for the note. Someone who is pushed into saying “stop” is a loss caused by the note. The decision uses those extras, not the raw chance that a purchase happens.

What “closed loop” means here

The two house rules stay the same every day: about 1.5% extra “stop,” and about 30% of people on one channel. The charges used to meet those rules do not stay the same. Each morning the opt-out charge and the channel charges are moved until the chosen notes sit on the rules, and the next morning starts from those new charges. That is the closed loop. Fatigue moves as well: yesterday’s note leaves the person more tired, and tomorrow’s score is computed at that new tiredness.

Methods

The charts use scikit-learn for the predictors and NumPy for the charge search. The charge search is a loop in this project. It does not call an outside solver.

Trees, on the prepared charts. The buy chance and the stop chance are histogram gradient-boosted trees, HistGradientBoostingRegressor. There is one group for each message and each answer: silence, email, push, and audio, once for a buy and once for a stop. A group has 40 trees, at most 3 questions deep, with at least 50 people in a leaf. The loss is squared error. Greedy predictive is one more of these trees, trained on the raw buy chance.

Other predictors. The same rows were also fit with a linear support-vector machine (LinearSVR), a kernel map (Nystroem) in front of that machine, and a small neural net (MLPRegressor). A second run used log loss: logistic regression, boosted-tree classifiers, and MLPClassifier. Those log-loss fits stay recorded experiments. Simulate custom can switch the live scores to the support-vector machine, the neural net, or the small copy. The prepared charts stay with the boosted trees. The small copy names the tree's message at the caps on the sliders and does not tune a charge. Its Qini curve stays on the trees.

Charge search. Each pass gives every person the message with the best score. The opt-out charge then moves by 2 times the gap between predicted extra stops and the cap. Each channel charge moves by 0.12 times the gap between that channel's share and its cap. A charge stays at or above 0. A morning takes 80 passes. Each morning of the 21-day run starts from yesterday's charges. Two sorts then move whole people back inside the caps.

The small copy. Distillation trains a boosted-tree classifier, HistGradientBoostingClassifier, depth 6, to name the message the full rule assigned. That rule is the trees' extra buy and extra stop, then the message chosen at a 1.5% opt-out cap and a 30% channel cap. The 21-day comparison is under Results, on Distillation.

Setup. The held-out people. Tiredness starts at 0. Each morning a rule picks one message. One draw between 0 and 1 is compared with the chance under the chosen message and with the chance under silence, so one person contributes +1, 0, or −1. Extra buy is the average of the buy gaps. Weekend shift is 1.8 and the horizon is 21 days. Tiredness carries into the next morning. Simulate custom is where those knobs can move.

Benchmarks. Two rules. On Monday they send the same messages.

  1. Closed-loop uplift. The score from the policy section. Each morning starts from yesterday's opt-out charge and channel charges, then moves them for today's tiredness. On a weekend, predicted extra stops are multiplied by the traffic shift before the charge moves.
  2. Frozen charge. Monday's opt-out charge is kept for every later morning. After Monday the score is extra buy minus that frozen charge times extra stops, and the channel cap is applied. The weekend multiplier is left off this score.

Setup. Twenty-one mornings, on the held-out people. Everyone starts at tiredness 0, and each rule carries its own tiredness. A point's height is the average extra buys, and its horizontal position is the average extra stops, both versus always staying silent. Two charts hold the channel cap, at 15% and at 30%, and draw closed-loop at four opt-out caps: 0.5%, 1%, 2.5%, and 5%. A menu on the charts turns the weekly limit off. With the limit on, a person can receive at most 3 notes in 7 days.

Benchmarks. Five rules. Closed-loop uplift is four points and each other rule is one point.

  1. Random. A message drawn at random among email, push, and audio, then trimmed to the channel caps. Anyone left over stays silent.
  2. Static heuristic. A fixed rule with no trees. A push when the last buy was three months ago or longer, and email otherwise, again inside the channel caps.
  3. Greedy predictive. Rank by the raw buy chance, with no subtraction of silence, and message the top of the list inside the caps.
  4. Unconstrained uplift. Give each person the message with the largest extra buy. Both charges stay at 0, so the opt-out extra is ignored. A person whose largest extra buy is zero or negative stays silent. On this chart the channel cap still limits how many people receive each channel.
  5. Closed-loop uplift. The score and the charge passes, at that point's opt-out cap.

Predictors

The rows. The shopping cards cover about 64,000 people. The split is 56,000 rows to teach the predictor and 8,000 held out. The cards have no tiredness, no push, and no clip inside the app. We keep the card and append two draws. One draw is a tiredness. The other is a message, chosen at random from silence, email, push, and audio. A formula we wrote then gives a buy chance, and a second formula gives a stop chance. A coin flip turns each chance into a yes or a no. The predictor sees the card, the tiredness, the message, and those two answers. It does not see the chance itself. There is one fit for each message and each answer, so eight fits, plus one more fit for the raw buy chance. The prepared charts use histogram gradient-boosted trees: a sequence of small decision trees, each one correcting the misses left by the trees before it. The table below also scores a support-vector machine and a neural net on the same rows.

Recall, Precision & Accuracy

Each method names a chance for the message stored on the row. A chance at or above the cut-off is called a yes. The three cut-offs are 20%, 35%, and 50%. A lower cut-off keeps more of the people who bought or stopped, and it also calls more of the others a yes. Recall is the share of the real buyers, or stoppers, that the cut-off kept. Precision is the share of the yes calls that were right. Accuracy is the share of all people labeled correctly. A dash means the method called nobody a yes, so precision has no calls to judge. Train is the 56,000 rows the fit saw. Test is the 8,000 held out.

Each method names a buy chance and a stop chance for silence, email, push, and audio. The extra is the chance for a note minus the chance for silence. The row uses the note with the largest extra. A person is messaged when that extra is at or above the cut-off. The three cut-offs are 0, 10 points, and 20 points. Recall is the share of people whose real extra clears the cut-off and whom we still message. Precision is the share of the people we message whose real extra also clears the cut-off. Accuracy is the share of all people sorted correctly. Real extra is the average true extra among the people we message. A dash means the method messaged nobody. Train is the 56,000 rows the fit saw. Test is the 8,000 held out.

Shadow price and weekend shift

Purpose of test. When tiredness changes from one morning to the next, does starting from yesterday's opt-out charge and moving it again keep extra stops near the cap, compared with keeping Monday's charge for all 21 days?

Setup. Saturday and Sunday are busier. More people are around, so the same note is more likely to make someone say stop. The 1.8 is a setting we typed in for that busier day. A week of identical Wednesdays would never ask the charge to react. Tiredness is the other change from one morning to the next. Both rules carry it for 21 mornings.

The stop formula adds about 0.59 on a weekend before it turns the score into a chance. That 0.59 is log 1.8. A rested person who gets a push goes from about a 9% chance of saying stop on a Wednesday to about 15% on Saturday. The buy chance is the same every day of the week. The trees were trained on weekday rows, so their predicted extra stops are weekday numbers. The closed-loop rule multiplies those predictions by 1.8 before the charge moves, then starts the next morning from the charge it just finished with. Sunday starts from Saturday's higher charge. On Monday the bump is gone and the charge can come back down. The frozen rule keeps Monday's opt-out charge and plans the day with the weekday predictions. The horizontal axis is the day. Cyan and orange, on the left, are that morning's extra stops from a fresh yes-or-no draw, using the weekend stop chance on Saturday and Sunday. Purple, on the right, is the opt-out charge. Red is the cap. The four cases change only the opt-out cap and the channel cap.

Key takeaway. On a wide channel, a frozen Monday price leaves part of a tight allowance unused. On the tight channel the two prices spend about the same. A frozen zero still overshoots later.

Distillation

Purpose of test. The student copies the whole morning rule. Can that copy, used on its own to pick the message, stay close to the standard rule over 21 mornings?

What the student copies. The gradient-boosted trees learn an extra buy and an extra stop for each message. They do not pick the message. The teacher does. On the training rows the teacher searches for the opt-out charge and the channel charges that meet a 1.5% opt-out cap and a 30% channel cap, then gives each person the winning message. The student reads the shopping card and the tiredness and is trained to name that message.

Setup. The held-out people. Twenty-one mornings. Weekend shift is 1.8. Tiredness starts at 0 and carries forward. The caps are the ones the student copied: 1.5% opt-out and 30% channel. The standard policy scores today's tiredness with the gradient-boosted trees, then starts from yesterday's charges and moves them. The student names a message from the card and today's tiredness. The channel cap and the weekly skip are applied to that name. The student does not search for a new charge. Extra buys and extra stops are the average gap against silence, from a fresh coin each morning. The message columns are the average share of people on that message.

Segment note mixes

Purpose of test. For a whole segment, how often does the charged note match unconstrained uplift, and how often does it match the raw buy chance? Each segment is read with the channel cap off, then with the channel cap on.

Setup. Every held-out person, one morning. People are grouped by segment and split at tiredness 1.5. A segment is Sure Thing, Persuadable, Lost Cause, or Sleeping Dog. The assignment is written in Problem Formulation, under Notation, from past spend, months since the last purchase, and whether the person is new. Each mix cell is the percentage of that group. The segment menu shows one segment at a time. The channel-cap menu shows that segment with the cap off, or with the cap on. Under tiredness, the percentage is that group's share of the test population. λ is the opt-out charge solved for that scenario.

Unconstrained uplift. A message is scored by its extra buy. The opt-out charge stays at 0. With the channel cap off, each person receives the message with the largest extra buy. Silence wins when every extra buy is zero or negative. That is the same rule with the channel limit removed. With the channel cap on, people are ordered by that best extra buy, and each channel fills until it reaches the cap. A person whose best channel is full receives the next-best message. A person whose best extra buy is zero or negative stays silent. When every channel still has room, the two bands hand out the same notes.

Charged. The score subtracts λ times extra stops from the extra buy. Silence scores 0. With the channel cap off, each person keeps that winning note. With the channel cap on, a charge is added for a full channel, and some people move to their next note or to silence.

Greedy predictive. The raw buy chance is used as it stands. With the channel cap off, each person receives the message with the highest raw chance. With the channel cap on, the highest raw chances fill each channel, and a person whose channel is full receives the next message that still has room. Anyone left without a channel stays silent.

Note match. This is the share of the group who receive the same message. One percentage compares the charged note with unconstrained uplift. The other compares the charged note with greedy predictive.

Key takeaways

A note survives the charge only when its extra buy clears its extra stops. A push fails that test for Persuadable people, so the charged rule replaces it with audio or silence. Audio already clears it for Sure Things, so the charged rule keeps it. Unconstrained uplift and greedy predictive never apply that test, so both stay on the push.

Greedy predictive spends a scarce channel on people who would buy anyway. The charged rule spends it on people a note can still move, then picks the cheaper note. At a 1.5% opt-out cap and a 15% channel, greedy predictive holds the slots for rested Sure Things and leaves every tired Persuadable person silent. Unconstrained uplift still reaches 64% of rested Persuadable people with a push. The charged rule reaches them with email, and leaves more of them silent. Sleeping Dogs show the same split with the cap off: greedy predictive emails them, and the charged rule leaves most of them silent.

A higher opt-out price quiets the charged note, and a wider channel can raise that price. At a 1.5% cap the price is 0.69 with a 15% channel and 0.71 with a 30% channel. At a 5% cap and a 15% channel the opt-out price falls to 0. Channel prices of 0.04 on email and 0.08 on push hold those channels full, and with the cap off the charged note matches unconstrained uplift. At a 5% cap and a 30% channel the opt-out price is 0.29, and a push still leads rested Persuadable people.
